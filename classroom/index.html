First-Then Board in the Classroom: Teacher Guide

Last updated: November 26, 2026

In a classroom, a First-Then board shows a student two things: the work to do now and the good thing that comes after it. Keep it where the student can see it, use the same short words every time, and give the Then as soon as the work is done.

Why use a First-Then board at school?

A school day is full of changes, and a spoken instruction is easy to miss in a busy room. A board with two pictures stays in front of the student. They can look at it again without asking.

A First-Then board is a visual support. When the National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice reviewed the research in 2020, visual supports were one of the 28 evidence-based practices it named for autistic children and young people. One training guide from the University of North Carolina calls the tool a first/then schedule: two events shown in a row, so that the student understands what comes next (see Sources).

Where should the board be?

Put it where the student looks while they work. A board on the wall across the room helps much less.

  • On the desk. A printed board taped to the desk, or a tablet standing next to the work.
  • In your hand. A small printed board that you or the classroom aide can carry from the classroom to the gym or the lunchroom.
  • On the big screen. For the whole class, open the board on the classroom screen and use full screen, so only the two cards show.

You can print a blank board and add pictures by hand, or make the board on this site and show it on a tablet.

How do I introduce it to a student?

Pick a quiet lesson and work the student can already do. At the start the point is not the work. The point is that the student sees the board keep its promise.

  1. Choose an easy First. A short task that usually goes well, like “Write your name.”
  2. Choose a strong Then. Ask the student or the family what the student likes. Two minutes with a favorite thing is enough.
  3. Point and say it. Touch each card as you speak: “First write your name, then blocks.” Keep the sentence short.
  4. Give the Then right away. The reward comes when the task is done, not at the end of the lesson.
  5. Repeat. Use it a few times a day. When the easy tasks work, put harder work on the First card.

What can I put on the cards at school?

Put one clear piece of work on the First card and a short, real reward on the Then card.

  • Desk workFirst write your name, then blocks · First five math problems, then computer · First read one page, then drawing
  • Moving aroundFirst line up, then hold the door · First walk in the hall, then gym · First coat on, then bus
  • Group timeFirst sit on the carpet, then song · First listen to the story, then puzzle · First wait for your turn, then game
  • RoutinesFirst clean up, then snack · First wash hands, then sticker · First pack your bag, then free time

Can I use it with the whole class?

Yes. One board on the big screen can show the plan for everyone: “First clean up, then story.” It can help the whole group at times of change, and the student who needs pictures does not stand out.

A student who needs more help can also have their own board on the desk, with a task and a reward that fit them.

How do we keep it the same for all adults?

Agree on the pictures and the words, and use them the same way. A board is easier to follow when every adult says the same short sentence.

  • Show the board to the classroom aide, the therapist and the substitute teacher.
  • Send the same pictures and words home (a printed blank board filled in by hand works), and ask the family which pictures and words they use.
  • If the student understands photos better than icons, use photos of the real things in your classroom. The training guide lists these forms for a visual support: real objects, photographs, drawings or picture symbols, and written words.

What are common mistakes at school?

A common mistake is a reward that comes too late. “Then” must mean now, not after lunch.

  • Too much work on First. “Finish the worksheet” can be too big. “Do the first three” is one clear task.
  • Lunch or recess as the reward. Do not put anything on the Then card that the student gets anyway.
  • Adding more work. When the First is done, the Then comes, with no extra task in between.
  • Using it as a warning. Show the board before the work starts, not after a problem as a way to take something away.
  • Only for hard lessons. Use it for work the student likes too, so the board does not come to mean trouble.

What comes after First-Then?

When two cards work well most of the time, add a third card with a First-Then-Next board, and later a visual schedule for the whole day. For longer work, a token board can help. The guide First-Then board vs visual schedule vs token board explains when to use which.
